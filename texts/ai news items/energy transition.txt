The Great Energy Shift: How the World Is Slowly Turning Away from Coal and Oil

As scientists warn that the window to limit global warming is narrowing, governments, utilities and investors around the world are racing to rebuild the way electricity is made and used. The shift, known as the energy transition, is reshaping economies, creating new industries and putting pressure on some of the world's most powerful fossil fuel producers.

At its core, the transition is a move away from coal, oil and natural gas toward cleaner sources such as wind, solar, hydropower and nuclear. The reason is simple: burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, the main driver of human-caused climate change. Energy production accounts for roughly three-quarters of global greenhouse gas emissions, making it the single most important sector in the effort to slow warming.

Just a decade ago, renewable energy was widely considered too expensive to compete without heavy subsidies. That picture has changed dramatically. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the cost of electricity from solar panels has fallen by more than 70 percent since 2010, while onshore wind has dropped by around a quarter. In many sunny and windy regions, new renewable plants are now cheaper to build than coal or gas plants, a trend analysts once thought was decades away.

The falling costs have been matched by rapid growth. Wind and solar capacity has expanded year after year, and renewables now account for a growing share of new power generation worldwide. China, the world's largest emitter, is also the largest producer of solar panels and a leading installer of wind turbines. In Europe, Germany's "Energiewende" policy has pushed renewables past a third of the country's electricity supply, while Denmark regularly generates more than half of its power from wind.

Still, the transition is far from complete. Fossil fuels continue to supply around 80 percent of the world's primary energy, and global demand for energy keeps rising as populations grow and developing economies industrialize. Coal remains a particular concern. While plants are closing in Britain, Canada and parts of the United States, new coal projects are still being built in Asia, where cheap power is seen as essential to lifting millions out of poverty.

Experts say the central challenge is no longer just generating clean electricity but managing it. Wind and solar output rises and falls with the weather, which means grids must find ways to keep the lights on when the sun sets or the wind drops. Battery storage, once prohibitively expensive, is becoming more affordable thanks to advances in lithium-ion technology and the growth of the electric vehicle market. Utilities are also investing in smarter grids, better long-distance transmission lines and systems that adjust demand in real time.

Transport and heating present further hurdles. Cars, trucks, ships and aircraft burn vast quantities of oil, and many homes and factories still rely on natural gas. Electric vehicles are gaining ground, with sales climbing in Norway, China and the United States, but they still make up a small fraction of cars on the road. Heavy industries such as steel and cement are harder to clean up and may require new technologies such as hydrogen or carbon capture.

The human impact of the transition is also drawing attention. Communities built around coal mining and oil drilling fear job losses and economic decline. Labor unions and policymakers increasingly speak of a "just transition," arguing that workers and regions dependent on fossil fuels should receive retraining, investment and support as industries change. Meanwhile, the renewable sector already employs millions of people worldwide, and its workforce is expected to keep growing.

Under the 2015 Paris Agreement, nations pledged to keep global temperature rise well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. Yet current national commitments fall short of that goal, according to United Nations assessments. Scientists say emissions would need to fall sharply within the next decade to avoid the most damaging effects, including rising seas, more intense heat waves and threats to food and water supplies.

For many observers, the direction of travel is clear even if the pace is not. Cheaper technology, public pressure and economic opportunity are all pushing in the same direction. Whether the world can move fast enough, however, remains the defining question of the energy transition.

Extreme Weather Is Becoming the New Normal, Scientists Say

Heat waves, heavy downpours and prolonged droughts are growing more frequent and more intense across much of the world, and climate scientists say the trend is closely tied to rising levels of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. As the planet continues to warm, researchers warn that what was once considered unusual weather is quickly becoming routine.

The global average temperature has climbed by about one degree Celsius since the late 1800s, according to several independent datasets maintained by government agencies and universities. While that number may sound small, scientists say it represents an enormous amount of additional energy in the Earth's climate system. That energy does not distribute itself evenly. Instead, it shows up as stronger heat waves, wetter storms and longer dry spells.

"Think of it as loading the dice," said one atmospheric scientist who studies extreme weather. "We still have natural variability, but the odds are shifting toward more severe outcomes."

The basic physics is well understood. For every degree Celsius of warming, the atmosphere can hold roughly seven percent more water vapor. That extra moisture can fuel heavier rainfall when storms develop, increasing the risk of flash flooding and river overflow. At the same time, higher temperatures speed up evaporation from soil and plants, which can intensify droughts in regions that are already dry.

Heat is perhaps the clearest example of the change. Records show that the number of record-high temperatures set each year now far exceeds the number of record lows in many countries. Public health officials say extreme heat is among the deadliest weather hazards, particularly for older adults, young children and people without access to air conditioning. Cities, which absorb and hold heat in pavement and buildings, are especially vulnerable, and urban planners are exploring ways to cool neighborhoods through tree planting, reflective roofing and expanded green space.

Oceans are also feeling the effects. More than 90 percent of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases has been absorbed by the seas, according to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Warmer water provides more fuel for tropical storms and can contribute to rising sea levels as it expands. Higher seas mean that coastal flooding, even during ordinary high tides, is becoming more common in low-lying communities.

Researchers have developed a field known as attribution science to measure how much climate change influences individual weather events. By running computer simulations of the climate with and without human-caused emissions, scientists can estimate how much more likely or severe an event became. Several studies in recent years have concluded that some heat waves would have been nearly impossible without human influence on the climate.

Experts are careful to point out that climate change does not cause every storm or drought, and that natural patterns such as El Niño continue to play a major role in year-to-year weather. But they stress that the long-term direction is clear. "We are not talking about a distant future," said a climate analyst at a major research institute. "The changes are already measurable and already affecting people."

The financial consequences are mounting. Insurance companies report that weather-related disasters have cost the global economy hundreds of billions of dollars over the past decade. Farmers face greater uncertainty as growing seasons shift and extreme conditions threaten crops. Governments are increasingly investing in stronger levees, improved drainage systems and early warning networks to limit the damage.

Efforts to slow the pace of warming remain a subject of intense debate. Under the 2015 Paris Agreement, nearly 200 nations pledged to hold warming well below two degrees Celsius above preindustrial levels. However, analyses by the United Nations suggest that existing national commitments are not enough to meet that goal, and global carbon dioxide emissions have continued to rise in recent years.

Supporters of stronger action argue that moving toward renewable energy, improving efficiency and protecting forests could reduce risks while creating new jobs. Critics worry about the costs to businesses and consumers and argue that adaptation, rather than rapid emission cuts, should be the priority. Many experts say both approaches will be necessary.

Whatever path is chosen, scientists agree that the longer action is delayed, the harder it becomes to avoid the most severe impacts. For communities already coping with hotter summers and heavier rains, the message is increasingly urgent: the climate is changing, and preparing for its consequences can no longer wait.

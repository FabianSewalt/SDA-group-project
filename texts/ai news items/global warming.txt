Rising Seas and a Warming Planet: Coastlines Face an Uncertain Future

As global temperatures climb, the world's oceans are rising at a pace scientists say is accelerating, putting hundreds of millions of people who live near coastlines at increasing risk. From low-lying island nations to major cities such as Miami, Jakarta and Shanghai, communities are confronting a threat that experts say will shape the century.

The basic cause is well understood. Global average temperatures have risen by about 1 degree Celsius since the late 19th century, driven largely by the burning of fossil fuels and the resulting buildup of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Because the oceans absorb more than 90 percent of the excess heat trapped by those gases, the water itself is warming. Warm water expands, and that thermal expansion has been one of the two main drivers of sea level rise.

The second driver is melting ice. Glaciers in places such as the Alps, Alaska and the Himalayas are shrinking, and the vast ice sheets covering Greenland and Antarctica are losing mass at a faster rate than in previous decades. Satellite measurements show that Greenland alone has been shedding hundreds of billions of tons of ice each year. When that water flows into the sea, it adds directly to ocean volume.

According to data compiled by NASA and other agencies, global sea level has risen by roughly 20 centimeters, or about 8 inches, since 1900. The rate has not been steady. For much of the 20th century, the oceans rose by about 1.5 millimeters a year, but since the early 1990s, satellite records show the pace has more than doubled, to over 3 millimeters annually. Scientists say it is continuing to speed up.

Projections for the future vary, largely because of uncertainty over how quickly the polar ice sheets will respond. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has said that if greenhouse gas emissions remain high, the global average sea level could rise by about a meter by 2100. Some researchers warn that rapid ice loss from Antarctica could push that figure higher. Even if emissions are sharply reduced, seas are expected to keep rising for centuries because oceans and ice sheets respond slowly to changes in temperature.

The effects are already visible. In many coastal cities, so-called "nuisance flooding" during high tides has become far more common. In parts of the southeastern United States, streets flood on sunny days when tides run unusually high. Storm surges, such as those that accompany hurricanes and typhoons, are riding on higher baseline water levels, making their impact more destructive. Saltwater is also seeping into freshwater supplies and farmland, threatening drinking water and crops in places such as the Mekong Delta and the Nile Delta.

Small island nations are among the most vulnerable. In the Pacific, countries such as Kiribati, Tuvalu and the Marshall Islands sit only a few meters above sea level. Their leaders have warned that rising waters could make parts of their territory uninhabitable, forcing difficult decisions about relocation and the loss of cultural heritage. Some governments have already begun purchasing land abroad or planning for the migration of their populations.

Cities and governments are beginning to respond. The Netherlands, long accustomed to living below sea level, has invested heavily in dikes, storm barriers and floodplain management. New York, London and Tokyo are building or planning flood defenses, while some communities are considering the more painful option of "managed retreat," moving homes and infrastructure away from the water's edge. Such measures are expensive, and experts note that poorer nations often lack the resources to protect themselves.

Scientists emphasize that the scale of future sea level rise depends heavily on choices made now. Cutting greenhouse gas emissions can slow the rate of warming and reduce the risk of the most extreme outcomes. Adaptation, meanwhile, will be necessary regardless, since some degree of further rise is already locked in.

For the roughly 600 million people living in coastal areas less than 10 meters above sea level, the issue is not abstract. As the oceans continue to warm and expand, the question is no longer whether the seas will rise, but how fast, and how well the world will be prepared.

Warming Planet Puts the World's Food Supply Under Pressure

Farmers around the globe are confronting a problem that no amount of hard work can solve on its own: the climate they have relied on for generations is changing, and the crops they grow are struggling to keep up.

Agriculture sits at a difficult crossroads in the climate debate. It is one of the sectors most vulnerable to rising temperatures, shifting rainfall and extreme weather, and at the same time it is a significant contributor to the greenhouse gases driving those changes. According to estimates from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, agriculture, forestry and other land use together account for roughly a quarter of global greenhouse gas emissions. Livestock, rice paddies, fertilizer use and the clearing of forests for farmland all play a part.

The effects on food production are already visible. Scientists say that for every degree Celsius of warming, yields of staple crops such as wheat, rice and maize can decline by several percent. Heat stress during key growth stages, such as flowering, can sharply cut the amount of grain a plant produces. Warmer nights, which have been increasing in many regions, force crops to burn through energy reserves that would otherwise go toward growth.

Water is another major concern. In many parts of the world, rainfall patterns are becoming less predictable. Some regions are experiencing longer dry spells, while others are seeing heavier downpours that wash away topsoil and flood fields. Farmers in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, many of whom depend on seasonal rains rather than irrigation, are especially exposed. A late monsoon or a failed rainy season can mean the difference between a good harvest and a hungry year.

"Farmers are used to dealing with variability, but what we are seeing now goes beyond what they have had to manage in the past," said one agricultural economist who studies climate adaptation. "The margin for error is getting smaller, particularly for smallholders who have little financial cushion."

Those smallholders matter enormously. The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization estimates that small farms produce a large share of the food consumed in developing countries, and many of the families running them already live close to the edge of poverty. When a harvest fails, they often have no insurance, limited savings and few alternatives.

The picture is not uniform. Some northern regions, including parts of Canada and Russia, may see longer growing seasons as temperatures rise, potentially opening new land to cultivation. Higher concentrations of carbon dioxide can also boost the growth of some plants in a process known as carbon fertilization. But researchers caution that these gains are likely to be offset by other factors, including heat, drought, pests and diminished nutritional quality. Studies have suggested that crops grown under higher carbon dioxide levels may contain less protein, zinc and iron, a worrying prospect for populations that rely on grains for most of their nutrition.

Pests and diseases are expected to complicate matters further. Warmer conditions allow insects to survive winters that once kept their numbers in check, and to expand into new territory. Farmers in several countries have reported crop-damaging pests appearing earlier in the season or in places where they were previously unknown.

Efforts to adapt are under way. Plant breeders are developing varieties of wheat, rice and other crops that tolerate heat, drought or salty soils. Farmers are experimenting with techniques such as conservation tillage, crop rotation and cover crops, which can improve soil health and help it retain moisture. Drip irrigation and better water management can stretch limited supplies. In some regions, farmers are shifting planting dates or switching to different crops better suited to the new conditions.

Agriculture can also be part of the solution. Practices that store more carbon in soil, reduce fertilizer waste and improve manure management could lower emissions while sometimes boosting productivity. Cutting food waste, which the United Nations says accounts for roughly a third of all food produced, would ease pressure on land and resources as well.

Yet experts say the scale of the challenge is daunting. With the global population projected to approach 10 billion by 2050, demand for food is expected to rise substantially even as the climate becomes less forgiving. Meeting that demand will require investment in research, support for farmers in poorer countries and a serious effort to curb the emissions that are driving the changes in the first place.

For the people who grow the world's food, the message is clear: the weather is no longer something that can be taken for granted.

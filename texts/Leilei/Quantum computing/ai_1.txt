IBM Opens Its Most Advanced Quantum Computer in Chicago as Industry Pushes Toward Practical Use

IBM has switched on its most advanced quantum computer to date at the University of Chicago's Hyde Park Labs. It's a big step in the city's plan to become a global centre for quantum technology. The machine, an IBM Quantum System Two, went live on November 6 and is built around the company's Nighthawk processor, a chip with 120 superconducting qubits.

Unlike many earlier quantum installations, which served mainly as laboratory testbeds, the Chicago system is explicitly aimed at turning research into practical applications. IBM said early work will focus on drug discovery and the optimisation of electrical power grids, two fields in which classical computers struggle to model the enormous number of possible configurations involved.

Several organisations have already signed on as early partners. They include the consulting firm Deloitte, the pharmaceutical company AbbVie, the Electric Power Research Institute and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. Together they represent a mix of commercial users and academic researchers, a combination that IBM hopes will help identify problems where quantum machines can deliver a genuine advantage.

The launch builds on years of regional investment. Illinois has positioned itself as a quantum hub through a quantum algorithms centre established in 2024, with IBM as a key member, and the Illinois Quantum and Microelectronics Park. In total, around 750 million dollars in state and private money has been committed to the effort. IBM is also expanding its training programmes in the city: its FutureNow Chicago centre, run in partnership with City Colleges of Chicago, is expected to create 750 jobs in artificial intelligence, cybersecurity and quantum computing.

The Chicago announcement arrives during a busy period for the quantum industry. Several companies reported strong growth in their latest quarterly results. IonQ said its third-quarter revenue reached 173.8 million dollars, more than four times the figure a year earlier, and raised its full-year forecast. Quantinuum reported that its Helios system had achieved a logical fidelity of 99.9995 percent, an error rate ten times lower than its previous record. In Finland, IQM announced 42.2 million euros in new orders, including a 33 million euro contract linked to the LUMI AI Factory.

Meanwhile, Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee launched a platform connecting IBM's Nighthawk processor with a supercomputer powered by AMD chips. The project reflects a growing belief that quantum and classical machines will work side by side, each handling the tasks it does best, rather than one replacing the other.

Despite the progress, significant obstacles remain. Quantum bits are extremely fragile, and even small disturbances from heat or electromagnetic noise can corrupt a calculation. Much of the industry's research therefore focuses on error correction, techniques that combine many physical qubits into more reliable logical qubits. A recent independent analysis of IBM's error-correcting memory circuits found that their protective strength had been slightly overestimated in earlier work, a reminder that claims in the field still require careful verification.

Researchers generally agree that large-scale, fully reliable quantum computers are still several years away. Yet installations like the one in Chicago show that the technology is moving out of the physics laboratory and into settings where businesses can begin testing it on real problems. For now, the question is less whether quantum computers will matter than how soon they will deliver results that classical machines cannot match.
